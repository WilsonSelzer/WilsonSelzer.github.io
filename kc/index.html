Which Kansas City small businesses are ready to sell, and who's already buying them?

I went to high school in Kansas City. In 2026 I built acquisition-target data for a PE-backed senior care roll-up, and saw how much of a deal team's week goes into simply finding businesses and guessing which owners might sell.

Most of that information is public. It's just scattered across government loan records, map listings, company websites and federal filings. That led me to a simple question: using only free public data, how close can you get to a target list a PE firm would actually use?

The result maps every independent business in 13 common roll-up industries around Kansas City, flags the owners most likely to sell, shows which ones a platform already owns, and refreshes itself every month.

Every business comes from Overture Maps, an open directory backed by Amazon, Meta and Microsoft. Each one is enriched with SBA 7(a) and 504 loan history since 2010, details read from its own website, and retirement-plan headcounts from Department of Labor Form 5500 filings. Franchises and chains are removed, since roll-ups buy independents.

  1. Consolidation is already underway in vet care. About 1 in 10 Kansas City vet clinics' websites name a PE-backed platform: Mission Pet Health (16 clinics), National Veterinary Associates (10) and Thrive Pet Healthcare (4).
  2. Deal sizes have doubled. The median SBA loan used to buy a KC business rose from $479,000 in FY2019 to $1.0 million in FY2026, with 58 purchases financed in FY2026 alone.
  3. Dental is ripe for succession deals. Of the 119 KC dental offices with SBA loans, 73 first borrowed 10 or more years ago, a rough sign the owner is further along in their career.
  4. Hundreds of owners have been at it for decades. 707 businesses say on their websites they've operated since 1996 or earlier.
  5. Auto repair is the most fragmented market. 2,150 independent shops and car washes, with 21 bought by new owners using SBA loans since 2018.
  1. Collect. A scheduled GitHub job runs every month. It pulls Overture's business listings for the metro and checks SBA's site for a new quarterly loan file.
  2. Clean. It sorts businesses into 13 categories, removes franchises and closed listings, merges duplicate locations, and matches listings to SBA borrowers by name and location.
  3. Enrich. It visits each business's website for founding year, family ownership, hiring and any mention of a parent platform; looks up when the website domain was registered; and matches Form 5500 filings for headcount.
  4. Serve. The map, the business profiles and the Excel export all read from the refreshed files, so the tool stays current with no manual work.

This reflects public data as published. Map listings can be out of date, websites can overstate their history, and SBA records only cover businesses that borrowed through SBA programs. "Long-time owner" and "years in business" are rough signals, not owner ages. The ownership flag only catches platforms a website names, so it will miss quiet acquisitions. Revenue isn't public for private companies; headcount only exists for businesses that file a Form 5500.

The point isn't a perfect list. It's to show how much of a sourcing team's first pass can be automated from free data, so people can spend their time on calls instead of spreadsheets.
